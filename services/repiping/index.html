Signs It Is Time to Plan a Repipe

One leak does not mean the whole house needs new pipe. A pattern does. Look for repeated pinhole leaks in different rooms, water that runs brown or orange when you first open a tap, pressure that has dropped slowly over the years, or green crust at copper joints. Galvanized steel pipe rusts from the inside, narrowing until a shower trickles.

A plumber confirms by looking at exposed pipe in the basement, crawlspace or water heater closet, checking pressure with a gauge, and asking about your repair history. EPA says ideal household pressure is 45 to 60 psi, and a reading well below that can point to a leak or a clogged line. If a leak is the real question, tracing the leak first may save you from replacing pipe that is fine.

PEX or Copper: Reading the Cost Guides Honestly

PEX is flexible plastic tubing that snakes through walls with fewer cuts and fittings. Copper is rigid metal pipe soldered at each joint. Neither is automatically right for every house. A plumber helps you choose based on your walls, your water and your budget.

HomeGuide's figures for a 2,000 square foot home show how far apart the materials land. It lists PEX at $2,000 to $4,000, with copper at $8,000 to $16,000. The Plastics Pipe Institute notes that potable PEX tubing carries a minimum extrapolated lifetime of 50 years in a standard chlorine-resistance test. That is a test criterion and not a promise about your house.

How a Repipe Unfolds, Step by Step

  1. A walk-through to trace the existing lines, count fixtures and note how the walls are built.
  2. A written plan and price, including where wall access will happen.
  3. Permit filed with the City if it is needed.
  4. Water shut off, old lines cut out and new pipe run to each fixture.
  5. Pressure test, inspection if required, then wall and ceiling patching.

Angi and Bob Vila report that a repipe can take a few days for a small home and several weeks for a large one. You may be without water during working hours. Most plumbers restore water at the end of each day, but ask about it when you schedule. If you have a basement or crawlspace, much of the new pipe can run there with fewer wall openings.

Cutting Into Walls and Making Them Whole

Access is the part homeowners dread. Plumbers cut small openings at the top and bottom of each wall run, fish new pipe through, and patch afterward. Rough drywall patches are common. Smooth paint-ready finishes are a separate trade, so ask what is included. Plaster walls in older houses are harder to patch than drywall.

Plan for it. Clear furniture from walls, empty cabinets under sinks, and cover floors. Tile is the biggest risk. A bathroom wall behind tile may need to be opened from the other side to avoid breaking it, and your plumber will tell you if that is possible. Water heater connections and shutoff valves are replaced during the same visit.

Permits and Inspection in Forest Grove

Under Oregon Building Codes Division rules, altering piping inside walls, ceilings or floors needs a permit, and so does work on water service or building sewers. The person doing the work gets it. In Forest Grove, the City Community Development office at 1924 Council St handles building and plumbing permits at 503-992-3229. Plan to start within 180 days of issue, or the permit expires.

The 2026 Oregon Plumbing Specialty Code took effect on October 1, 2026, and is based on the 2024 Uniform Plumbing Code. Your plumber builds to it. The City reports no lead or galvanized service lines needing replacement, but that is about service lines, not the pipe inside your walls. If your problem is on the line from the meter to the house, see water service line repair.

Repipe, Spot Repair or Replace a Section

A full repipe is not the only option. If leaks are limited to one bathroom or one run, replacing that section can be enough. Choose a partial replacement when the rest of the pipe tests sound. Choose a whole-house repipe when failures are spread out, the pipe material is known to fail, or repairs are coming every year.

If a pipe has already burst, our burst pipe repair page covers the immediate steps. Adding a new bathroom or moving a kitchen sink while walls are open is plumbing installation work, and it is smart to price it in the same visit. To talk through which fits your house, call (503) 974-3329.

Safety and prep steps before a repipe starts

A repipe is planned work, so a short checklist keeps the house safe and the day short. None of it needs tools, and most of it is about protecting people and belongings.

  1. Find your main shutoff valve at the house and make sure it turns. Do not touch the city water meter, because Forest Grove asks customers never to shut water off there.
  2. Clear a path to the walls, closets and cabinets where pipe will be opened, and move furniture and valuables out of those rooms.
  3. Plan for the water to be off for part of the day, and store drinking water and a few filled containers for flushing toilets.
  4. Keep children and pets out of the work areas, because open walls, tools and dust are hazards.
  5. Do not use the water heater while the lines are drained. A heater that fires with an empty tank can be damaged, so turn it off or to pilot as the plumber directs.
  6. Ask who handles the permit and the final inspection, since piping inside walls, ceilings and floors needs a permit in Oregon.

Ask your plumber to walk the house with you first so the access plan and the water-off window are agreed before anything is opened.

What a Repipe Costs and Why

Cost guides such as Angi report a national average of about $7,500 and a wide range of $1,500 to $15,000. Price swings with the size of the house, the material and how hard the walls are to open. We do not quote per square foot. You get a clear price before work starts.

Number of fixtures and floors

Each sink, tub and toilet needs a new feed, and two-story homes cost more than single-story.

Pipe material

As the HomeGuide figures show, PEX and copper land thousands of dollars apart.

Wall and ceiling access

Plaster, tile and finished basements slow the work and raise patching cost.

Permits and inspection

Permits are required for piping inside walls and add city fees and an inspection step.

Add-on work

Water heater hookups, new shutoffs or a pressure regulator ($400 on average per Angi for a PRV replacement) can be done in the same project.

Homeowner policies rarely pay to replace aging pipe because it is wear and tear. They may cover water damage from a sudden failure. If a leak triggered the repipe, keep your documentation and ask your insurer what they will cover.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a repipe take?

Cost guides such as Angi and Bob Vila report a few days for a small home and several weeks for a large one. Your plumber should give a schedule after seeing the walls, the number of fixtures and whether a permit and inspection are needed. Ask whether water is restored each evening.

Do I need a permit to repipe my house in Forest Grove?

Yes. Oregon requires a permit to alter piping inside walls, ceilings or floors, and the person doing the work gets it. The City of Forest Grove Community Development office at 503-992-3229 issues them. Work should not start before the permit is in place, and a permit expires if work has not begun within 180 days.

Is PEX or copper better for my house?

Neither is better in every case. PEX is cheaper and flexible, which means fewer openings in finished walls. Copper costs more in labor and material and is rigid. Ask your plumber to explain the tradeoffs for your walls, fixtures and budget. We do not make claims beyond what cost guides and manufacturers publish.

Will you leave holes in my walls?

Plumbers open walls to run pipe and then patch the openings. Whether the patch is rough or paint-ready depends on what is agreed up front, so ask. Plaster and tile need extra care. Work in a crawlspace or basement can reduce the number of openings inside living areas.

How do I know if my old pipes need replacing?

Repeated pinhole leaks, brown water on first draw, falling pressure and visible corrosion are the usual signs. EPA says ideal pressure is 45 to 60 psi. A plumber can look at exposed pipe and test the system before recommending a full repipe or a partial replacement.
