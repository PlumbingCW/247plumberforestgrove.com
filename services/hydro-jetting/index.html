Jetting gets oversold and misunderstood. It is excellent on grease and roots and wrong for some pipe. Here is how it works, when a cable is enough, which pipes should not be jetted, and why the camera comes first.

How a Jetter Works and Why Plumbers Reach for It

A jetter is a pump, a long hose and a nozzle with several small holes. Water is forced out of those holes at high pressure. Some jets point backward and pull the hose forward through the pipe, while others spray sideways to scrape the wall. The EPA describes jetting as aiming a high-pressure water stream at a blockage, the most common hydraulic cleaning technique.

Cable or Jetter: Choosing Without Overspending

Not every stoppage needs pressure. A hair plug or a lodged object is a cable job, covered on our page about clearing clogged drains, and costs less. Jetting earns its price when the pipe is coated, when a cable keeps breaking through but the line never runs right, or when roots have filled a long run.

Camera First: What We Need to See Before We Blast

A camera on a push cable shows what the pipe is made of, where it bends and whether it is intact, and a full camera inspection of the line can be booked on its own. Without it, jetting is guessing. High-pressure water does little harm to sound pipe, but it can widen a crack or drive debris into a joint that was barely holding.

A recording of the pipe is useful beyond the visit. If the damage turns out to be in the public right of way, Clean Water Services Field Operations at 503-547-8100 accepts video from a plumber or homeowner when someone asks them to look at a defect.

Grease Scale and Roots: Where Jetting Earns Its Price

Grease does not stay greasy. It cools, mixes with soap and food, and hardens into a layer that grows inward like a candle drip. It is common on busy kitchen lines and on lines serving restaurants.

Roots are the second big reason. They enter at a joint looking for moisture and then thicken into a mat. Forest Grove gets 46.72 inches of precipitation a year, and wet winters keep buried soil moist, which draws roots toward any small leak. Jetting with a cutting nozzle removes roots without a dig, but roots grow back, so plan on repeat cleaning or a repair if they keep returning.

Pipes That Should Not Take Pressure

About 48 percent of Forest Grove homes were built before 1980, and about 1,030 date from 1939 or earlier. Old pipe is not automatically weak, but old, corroded or cracked pipe is. Roots can leave thin spots. Joints can shift. A scale-thickened wall can hide a weak one.

What a Jetting Visit Looks Like Start to Finish

  1. A camera goes in to confirm material, depth of the blockage and pipe condition.
  2. A cable opens a path if the line is completely stopped, since the jetter needs water to flow.
  3. The nozzle is run to the clog and pulled back slowly, washing walls as it comes.
  4. A second camera pass confirms the line is clear, and you see the footage.

Keeping the Line Clear After Jetting

A freshly jetted line is clean, not permanent. Grease can return if habits stay the same. Clean Water Services asks residents not to pour grease down drains and offers a Freeze the Grease kit at 503-681-3678. It also asks everyone to keep wipes labeled flushable out of the toilet.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do you handle hydro jetting emergencies at night or on weekends in Forest Grove?

Yes. Our dispatch is 24/7, so you can call (503) 974-3329 any night or weekend. A licensed plumber is sent with a clear price before work starts. We do not promise an arrival time, but we do not hold your call until the next business day.

Can hydro jetting damage my pipes?

On sound pipe, jetting is safe when done at the right pressure. On cracked, collapsed or badly corroded pipe it can make things worse, which is why a camera goes in first. If the camera shows damage, a plumber should explain repair options before any pressure is used.

Is jetting better than snaking?

Neither wins everywhere. A cable is faster and cheaper for a single plug. Jetting cleans the full pipe wall and lasts longer on grease or scale. The camera usually settles it, because the picture shows whether the pipe is lined with buildup or blocked in one spot.

How long does jetting last?

It depends on the cause. If grease was the problem and household habits change, a jetted line can stay open for years. Roots and sags tend to come back because the underlying condition is still there. Ask for the video so you know what to watch.

Who pays if the blockage is in the public sewer?

The private lateral runs from the house to the public sewer connection and is the owner's responsibility. CWS may repair structural defects within the public right of way if shown video. A plumber can tell you where the pipe is clear and where the problem sits.
