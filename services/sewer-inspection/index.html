Sewer Inspection in Forest Grove, OR

Most of a sewer line is buried where nobody can see it, so owners find out about problems only when something backs up. A sewer inspection moves that discovery to a day you choose. A licensed plumber feeds a small waterproof camera through the pipe, records what it sees and explains it. To schedule a visit, call (503) 974-3329.

People book this for three main reasons: they are buying an older home, they have had the same drain clog more than once, or they want a baseline before a remodel or landscaping project. The sections below cover what the camera shows, how to read the findings and what to do with them.

What a Camera Inspection Tells You That Nothing Else Can

The EPA describes sewer camera inspection, often called CCTV, as the most commonly used technique for looking inside a sewer. The camera head sits on a flexible rod with lights, and a screen above ground shows the pipe wall as the head moves along. A counter records distance, so a problem can be located by footage from the cleanout.

That matters because symptoms lie. A slow drain might be hair or it might be roots. A wet patch in the yard might be a water leak or a sewer leak. A picture ends the guessing and keeps you from paying for a repair on the wrong pipe.

Buying a House Built Before 1980

Roughly 48 percent of Forest Grove homes, 4,376 units, were built before 1980, and about 1,030 date to 1939 or earlier. Pipe laid in those decades has had more time to shift, rust or collect roots. A house can look perfect upstairs and still have a lateral that needs work.

A standard home inspection does not usually include a camera run of the sewer, so ask your agent whether to add one during the inspection period, while terms can still change. The lateral, from the house to the public sewer connection, is the owner's duty under City Ordinance 52.03, so a buyer takes on any defect.

The historic neighborhoods south of downtown, such as Clark and Painter's Woods, show why. Homes built across many decades often tie newer plumbing into older runs.

How the Visit Runs From Start to Finish

  1. The plumber finds the cleanout or opens the line at a toilet or roof vent.
  2. The camera goes in toward the public sewer connection while the recording runs.
  3. The plumber pauses at joints, bends and any spot with water, roots or damage.
  4. A locator on the surface can mark where the camera is, so you know where a problem sits in the yard.
  5. You review the footage together, and the plumber answers questions before leaving.

Plan on a couple of hours for a typical house. Clear a path to the cleanout and give the plumber a place to set the monitor. If a cleanout is missing, say so when you schedule. A packed line may need drain cleaning first so the camera can pass.

What a Useful Inspection Report Contains

Footage alone is hard to read. A good report ties each observation to a distance and a plain description. If a plumber hands you only a video file, ask for the notes.

Reading the Findings Without Panic

Not every finding calls for a dig. Some are cosmetic, some are watch-and-wait and a few are urgent. This table sorts the usual ones.

Common camera findings and what they usually mean.
FindingWhat it isTypical next step
Fine roots at a joint.Roots seeking moistureScheduled cleaning, recheck in a year or two.
Heavy root massRoots filling the pipe.Cut roots, then likely repair the joint.
Belly or sagPipe has settled and holds water.Monitor, or reset the section if it keeps clogging.
Offset jointTwo pipe ends no longer line up.Repair, since offsets catch debris and let soil in.

Rust scale in iron pipe.Interior corrosionClean, then plan replacement if walls are thin.

A scaled or rooted line that still drains can often be handled by jetting the pipe on a schedule. A cracked or collapsed section points toward sewer line repair. The plumber should tell you which category your pipe falls in and what happens if you wait.

When the Problem Sits Near the Street

Clean Water Services may repair structural defects in a lateral where they occur in the public right of way. These include cracked or broken pipe, open or offset joints and root intrusion. A belly is a different matter and does not count as one. A plumber, homeowner or realtor can send CWS Field Operations a video at 503-547-8100 to request help.

That is another reason the recording matters. If the camera shows the defect near the street, ask the plumber to note the distance so you can show where it falls. Anything on your side of that line remains yours. A repair may need a plumbing permit and an erosion control permit, and the digging crew must call 811 first.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a sewer camera inspection take?

A typical house takes about one to two hours, including setup, the run through the line and a review of the footage. Longer lots, limited access, a packed line that needs clearing first or several branches can add time. Tell the dispatcher what you know so the plumber can plan.

Should I get a sewer inspection before buying an older house in Forest Grove?

It is a sound idea. About 48 percent of local homes were built before 1980, and the lateral is the owner's responsibility under City Ordinance 52.03. A recording shows roots, cracks or sags before you own them and can support a repair request or price talk.

Will the report tell me if the City or CWS is responsible?

A good report shows where defects sit by distance. CWS may repair structural defects in the part of a lateral inside the public right of way, but not a sag. The plumber can mark the footage, and you or your agent can send the video to CWS Field Operations.

Is a camera inspection the same as a clog clearing?

No. Clearing opens a blocked pipe. An inspection looks at the pipe, usually without cleaning it. If a clog stops the camera, the plumber may need to clear the line first, and that step is quoted separately, with a price you hear before work starts.

Does the inspection damage the pipe?

Not normally. The camera head is small and flexible and travels inside the pipe. On weak or collapsed pipe it can snag, and if it does the plumber stops and notes it. That stoppage is itself a finding, because it points to a section that needs attention.
