How to Fix a Tub or Shower Faucet Handle in Forest Grove

By the Forest Grove 24/7 Plumbing team. Updated October 15, 2026.

A handle that wobbles, spins freely or keeps dripping usually points to one small part, and you can often tell which one in a few minutes.

Call (503) 974-3329

If you want to fix a tub or shower faucet handle in Forest Grove, first work out which problem you have. A wobbly handle usually needs a screw tightened. A handle that spins without changing the water has a stripped stem or insert. A handle that drips after you shut it off needs a new cartridge or new washers and seats. Turn the water off before you take anything apart.

Most of these repairs are small, but the valve itself hides inside the wall, and that is where trouble starts. Below you will find how to read the symptoms, how to tell a cartridge valve from a compression valve, what a swap costs, and the point where it makes sense to call (503) 974-3329 instead of forcing a stuck part.

Three Handle Problems and What Causes Each

The handle is only the lever. What it turns is a stem or cartridge deep in the valve body, and the symptom tells you which part has given up. Sort yours using the table, then read the sections that match.

A drip that continues with the handle fully closed means water is getting past the sealing part. Tightening harder only wears it out faster.

Cartridge or Compression Valve Behind the Wall

Look at what you have. A tub or shower with one handle that moves in an arc or rotates to mix hot and cold usually has a cartridge. A tub with two separate handles, one hot and one cold, often has compression stems, though some two-handle sets use cartridges too.

A cartridge is a plastic or brass cylinder that slides into the valve body and is held by a clip or nut. It controls flow and temperature in one part. A compression stem is older. It screws a rubber washer down onto a seat, and every turn grinds that washer a little flatter.

Compression repairs mean a new washer and sometimes a new seat, while cartridge repairs mean a whole new cartridge.

Getting the Water Off Without Making a Bigger Mess

Tub and shower valves often have no shutoff of their own, so you may need to close the main shutoff valve at the house. In Forest Grove, never close the water at the city meter. The City's policy is that customers and contractors leave the meter alone. If the meter or line breaks after someone turns it, repairs are at the homeowner's expense.

  1. Close the main shutoff valve where the water line enters the house.
  2. Open a faucet at a lower level to drain pressure from the pipes.
  3. Open the tub or shower valve to confirm no water comes out.
  4. Plug the tub drain with a rag so a dropped screw cannot disappear.
  5. Lay a towel in the tub floor to protect the finish from tools.

If the main valve is stuck or leaks when you turn it, stop there. For a shutoff at the meter, call Forest Grove Public Works at 503-992-3258. The City turns it off for you, with no fee during business hours and a fee after hours.

If the handle will not come off or the valve will not shut, a plumber can take it from here.

Taking a Loose or Stripped Handle Apart

Work gently. Most handles hold on with one screw hidden under a small cap or behind a set screw on the side. Pry the cap with a thin blade, back out the screw and pull the handle straight off. If it sticks, wiggle it. Do not pry against the tile.

Under the handle sits a trim plate, called an escutcheon, held with two screws. Remove it and you will see the stem or the front of the cartridge. Rounded splines, the tiny grooves where the handle grips, mean the part is stripped.

Before pulling a cartridge, note which way the hot side faces, since one put in backward swaps hot and cold.

What a Cartridge Replacement Costs

Cost guides give a useful anchor. According to This Old House, tub and shower cartridge replacement averages about $300, with a typical range of $200 to $400. Treat that as a national number. Your price depends on the valve, the access and what the plumber finds when the handle comes off.

Old Valves, Old Walls and the Permit Question

Forest Grove has a lot of older housing. Census data for 2020 to 2024 shows a median year built of 1982, but about 48 percent of the 9,094 housing units, or 4,376, went up before 1980. Roughly 1,030 date from 1939 or earlier. Bathrooms in those homes can still hold original brass valves.

Original valves tend to fight back. Stems fuse to the body and parts for the exact model may no longer be made. Forcing a stuck stem can twist the pipe inside the wall.

On permits, repairing or replacing a faucet that is not concealed in a wall, or a shower head, needs no permit. Altering piping inside walls does. If the supply lines must be opened, ask Forest Grove Community Development at 503-992-3229.

Signs That Mean Stop and Call a Plumber

If the valve will not shut off at all and water keeps running, close the main shutoff valve at the house right away, never at the city meter, and call (503) 974-3329. We dispatch around the clock.

Also stop for a stem that spins but will not pull out, a soft spot on the wall near the valve, or green corrosion on the pipe.

A repair that fails twice is another sign. If a new cartridge drips again within weeks, the seat or valve body is probably damaged. Several failing valves in an old bathroom can also point to aging supply pipes, which is a repiping conversation. Sink and tub fixtures elsewhere in the house fall under faucet repair.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a permit in Forest Grove to replace a tub or shower faucet handle?

Not for the handle. Oregon guidance says repairing or replacing a faucet that is not concealed in a wall, or a shower head, needs no permit. Altering piping inside walls, ceilings or floors does. If your repair involves opening the wall to change the valve, call Forest Grove Community Development at 503-992-3229 to confirm.

Why does my shower handle drip after I turn it off?

Water is slipping past a worn part. In a cartridge valve, the seals inside the cartridge have hardened or scored. In a compression valve, the rubber washer has flattened or the brass seat has pitted. Tightening the handle harder usually makes it worse. The usual fix is a new cartridge or a new washer and seat.

Can I replace only the handle and leave the cartridge alone?

Yes, if the handle is cracked or its splines are stripped but the stem or cartridge turns normally and does not drip. Match the handle to the brand and spline count. If the stem splines are rounded too, a new handle will slip again.

Is it safe to keep using a loose handle?

For a short time, yes, as long as it still shuts the water off fully. Tighten the screw if you can. If it will not hold, or the valve drips, plan the repair soon so a small fix does not become a leak inside the wall.
